# What is radon?

**Quick answer:** radon is a radioactive gas that forms naturally when uranium, thorium and radium break down in rock, soil and groundwater. You can't see, smell or taste it. It moves up through the ground, gets into homes through cracks and gaps in the foundation, and can build up indoors. Breathing it for years raises your risk of lung cancer: EPA estimates radon causes about 21,000 lung cancer deaths in the U.S. each year, second only to smoking. Testing is the only way to know your home's level, and EPA recommends fixing a home at 4 picocuries per liter (pCi/L) or higher.

Last updated October 31, 2026. Every figure on this page comes from EPA, CDC, the National Cancer Institute, the World Health Organization, the USGS or Pennsylvania DEP, linked where it's used and listed under Sources. Pennsylvania county figures are our analysis of DEP's public test data. General information, not medical advice.

## Radon at a glance

- **What it is:** a natural radioactive gas from the breakdown of uranium, thorium and radium in rock, soil and groundwater ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/what-radon)).
- **Why it matters:** the second leading cause of lung cancer in the U.S. after smoking, and the leading cause among people who have never smoked ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon)).
- **Warning signs:** none. It has no color, smell or taste, and CDC notes most people have no symptoms until lung cancer is advanced ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html)).
- **Action level:** fix your home at 4 pCi/L or higher; consider fixing between 2 and 4 pCi/L ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/what-epas-action-level-radon-and-what-does-it-mean)).
- **In Pennsylvania:** DEP says about 40% of homes tested are above 4 pCi/L. In DEP's public test data, 37% of 2,644,033 basement tests were at or above 4 pCi/L ([our county analysis](https://radonroster.com/pa/radon-levels-by-county/)).
- **What to do:** test (a kit costs about $20 to $30, or [get a free one in PA](https://radonroster.com/free-radon-test-kit-pennsylvania/)), and if the result is high, hire a DEP-certified mitigator.

## Where does radon come from?

Radon forms when uranium, thorium or radium, which occur naturally in rock, soil and groundwater, break down ([EPA: What is Radon Gas?](https://www.epa.gov/radon/what-radon)). Radon is itself radioactive. It gives off alpha particles and has a half-life of 3.8 days, so it decays quickly into other radioactive elements ([EPA: Radionuclide Basics](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radon)). One of them is polonium, and the USGS explains that polonium formed from radon, both in the air and inside people's lungs, is what can damage lung tissue ([USGS](https://www.usgs.gov/faqs/what-radon)).

Everyone breathes some radon. EPA puts the average outdoor level at about 0.4 pCi/L and the average indoor level at about 1.3 pCi/L. Outdoors, radon spreads out in the open air. Indoors, it can collect, and EPA has found elevated levels in every state ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radon)).

**Units:** in the U.S., radon is measured in picocuries per liter of air (pCi/L). Most other countries use becquerels per cubic meter (Bq/m³). EPA's 4 pCi/L action level equals 148 Bq/m³ ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radon)). The World Health Organization suggests a national reference level of 100 Bq/m³, which works out to about 2.7 pCi/L ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/radon-and-health)).

## How does radon get into a house?

Mostly from the soil under it. The air pressure inside a house is usually lower than the pressure in the soil around the foundation, so the house acts like a vacuum and draws soil gas, radon included, in through any opening ([EPA Consumer's Guide to Radon Reduction](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_consumers_guide_to_radon_reduction.pdf)).

Common ways in, from EPA and WHO:

- cracks in concrete floors and walls, and the joint where the floor meets the wall
- construction joints and gaps in suspended floors
- gaps around pipes, cables and other service entries
- cavities inside walls and pores in hollow-block walls
- sumps and drains
- the water supply, mainly private wells

([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radiation/radionuclide-basics-radon); [WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/radon-and-health))

**Any type of house can have it.** CDC and EPA both say radon can build up in a new or old home, a sealed or drafty one, and a home with or without a basement ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html); [EPA Citizen's Guide](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)). NCI adds that levels can be higher in well-insulated, tightly sealed homes and in homes built on soil rich in uranium, thorium and radium ([NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/radon/radon-fact-sheet)).

**Lower floors run higher.** NCI says basements and first floors typically have the highest levels because they are closest to the ground. Pennsylvania's test data shows the same pattern: 37% of basement tests reported to DEP were at or above 4 pCi/L, against 20% of 396,546 first-floor tests ([our 2026 data report](https://radonroster.com/pa/radon-data-report-2026/)).

**Your neighbor's result doesn't tell you yours.** Homes next door to each other can have different indoor levels ([NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/radon/radon-fact-sheet)), and EPA says a neighbor's test is not a good predictor of your own ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)).

**Levels change over time.** Radon can vary from day to day and month to month ([NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/radon/radon-fact-sheet)). PA DEP says the best time to test is the colder months, when homes are closed up and radon is most likely to build up ([PA DEP](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/newsroom/2026-01-12-dep-encourages-pennsylvanians-to-test-their-homes-for-radon-during-radon-action-month)).

### Radon in well water

Radon can dissolve in groundwater. EPA says radon in water is not usually a problem when the source is surface water; it is more likely with groundwater such as a private well. Most of the risk from radon in water comes from radon released into the air when water is used for showering and other household tasks, and in most homes radon from soil is a much larger risk than radon from water. If you have a well, test the air first. Point-of-entry treatment removes radon before water reaches the taps ([EPA Citizen's Guide](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)). EPA's Safe Drinking Water Hotline is (800) 426-4791.

## Why is radon dangerous?

When you breathe radon in, the radioactive particles it decays into can get trapped in your lungs. As they keep breaking down they release small bursts of energy that can damage lung tissue and lead to lung cancer over a lifetime ([EPA Citizen's Guide](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)). WHO notes the particles settle on the cells lining the airways, where they can damage DNA ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/radon-and-health)).

- **Lung cancer is the known risk.** NCI calls it the only cancer proven to be associated with inhaling radon ([NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/radon/radon-fact-sheet)).
- **About 21,000 U.S. deaths a year.** EPA's estimate, with an uncertainty range of 8,000 to 45,000. About 2,900 of those deaths are among people who have never smoked ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon)). NCI cites a range of 15,000 to 22,000 ([NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/radon/radon-fact-sheet)).
- **Second only to smoking.** Radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the U.S. and the leading cause among non-smokers ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon)). The U.S. Surgeon General issued a national health advisory on radon in 2005 ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon)).
- **Worldwide.** WHO estimates radon causes 3% to 14% of lung cancers, depending on a country's average radon level and smoking rate, and that lung cancer risk rises by about 16% for every 100 Bq/m³ increase in long-term average radon ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/radon-and-health)).
- **It takes years.** Not everyone exposed to elevated radon will develop lung cancer, and the time between exposure and disease may be many years ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)).

CDC lists the factors that raise your risk: high radon in a home or building where you spend a lot of time, high radon in the part of the building you use most (often basements and lower levels), smoking now or in the past, and burning wood, coal or other fuels that add particles to the air. There isn't enough data to show whether children face a higher lung cancer risk from radon than adults, but they may receive higher doses because they breathe faster and their lungs differ in size and shape ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html)).

### Radon and smoking

Smoking and radon together carry a much higher risk than either alone. WHO estimates smokers are about 25 times more at risk from radon than non-smokers ([WHO](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/radon-and-health)). EPA's risk chart shows the difference at the same lifetime radon level:

| Lifetime radon level | Never smoked: about this many per 1,000 could get lung cancer | Smokers: about this many per 1,000 |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 20 pCi/L | 36 | 260 |
| 10 pCi/L | 18 | 150 |
| 8 pCi/L | 15 | 120 |
| 4 pCi/L (EPA action level) | 7 | 62 |
| 2 pCi/L | 4 | 32 |
| 1.3 pCi/L (average indoor level) | 2 | 20 |

Source: [EPA, Health Risk of Radon](https://www.epa.gov/radon/health-risk-radon), lifetime risk from EPA's 2003 Assessment of Risks from Radon in Homes (EPA 402-R-03-003).

EPA notes that a former smoker's risk may be lower than a current smoker's. CDC's free quit line is 1-800-QUIT-NOW ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html)).

## What radon level is safe?

There is no known safe level of radon. EPA recommends fixing your home if the level is 4 pCi/L or higher, and considering a fix between 2 and 4 pCi/L ([EPA: What is EPA's Action Level for Radon](https://www.epa.gov/radon/what-epas-action-level-radon-and-what-does-it-mean)).

The 4 pCi/L action level is a point at which EPA says to act, not a line between safe and unsafe. CDC says reducing radon always helps lower your risk, even below 4 pCi/L ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html)), and EPA says most homes can be brought down to 2 pCi/L or less ([EPA Citizen's Guide](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)). WHO's suggested reference level of 100 Bq/m³ (about 2.7 pCi/L) is lower than EPA's.

For what to do at each result, including when to retest and when to go straight to mitigation, see [what your radon test results mean](https://radonroster.com/radon-testing-pennsylvania/#what-do-my-radon-test-results-mean) in our Pennsylvania testing guide.

## How common is high radon in Pennsylvania?

PA DEP says Pennsylvania has one of the most serious radon problems in the country, with an estimated 40% of homes tested above EPA's action guideline of 4 pCi/L ([PA DEP Radon Division](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/radiation-protection/radon-division)). DEP attributes this to the state's geology and says any home in Pennsylvania can have high radon ([PA DEP, 2026](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/newsroom/2026-01-12-dep-encourages-pennsylvanians-to-test-their-homes-for-radon-during-radon-action-month)). For comparison, NCI says about 1 in 15 U.S. homes is at or above 4 pCi/L ([NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/radon/radon-fact-sheet)).

- Statewide, **37% of 2,644,033 basement tests** were at or above 4 pCi/L.

## How do I test for radon?

Testing is the only way to know your level ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/radon/about/index.html)). EPA recommends testing all homes below the third floor ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)). The short version for Pennsylvania:

- **Use a kit or hire a pro.** DIY kits cost about $20 to $30 ([PA DEP](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/radiation-protection/radon-division/radon-in-the-home)). If you have never tested, you may qualify for a [free radon test kit in Pennsylvania](https://radonroster.com/free-radon-test-kit-pennsylvania/). You can also hire a [DEP-certified tester](https://radonroster.com/pa/counties/), which DEP advises when buying or selling a home.
- **Test the lowest lived-in level.** DEP says to test in the basement or on the ground floor.
- **Short-term first.** Short-term tests run 2 to 90 days and long-term tests more than 90 days ([NCI](https://www.cancer.gov/about-cancer/causes-prevention/risk/substances/radon/radon-fact-sheet)). A long-term test better reflects your year-round average.
- **Test in the colder months** if you can, when homes are closed up ([PA DEP](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/newsroom/2026-01-12-dep-encourages-pennsylvanians-to-test-their-homes-for-radon-during-radon-action-month)).

Placement, closed-house conditions, short- versus long-term tests, real-estate testing and how to read your result are all covered in our [guide to radon testing in Pennsylvania](https://radonroster.com/radon-testing-pennsylvania/).

## What should I do if my radon level is high?

1. **Confirm the result.** If results are 4 pCi/L or higher, confirm with a second test as PA DEP describes on its [Radon in the Home](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/radiation-protection/radon-division/radon-in-the-home) page. For a first result between 4 and 8 pCi/L, DEP suggests a long-term test or another short-term test, and fixing the home if the average is still 4 or more.
2. **Hire a DEP-certified mitigator.** Pennsylvania law requires anyone doing radon testing, mitigation or lab analysis for the public to be DEP-certified ([25 Pa. Code Chapter 240](https://files.dep.state.pa.us/RadiationProtection/BureauOfRadiationProtection/BRPPortalFiles/Chapter_240_2015-04-23.pdf)). Find certified mitigators in the [county directory](https://radonroster.com/pa/counties/), or [get free quotes](https://radonroster.com/quotes/).
3. **Expect a vent-pipe system.** The most common method is soil suction: a pipe and fan pull radon from under the foundation and vent it above the roof. EPA says systems can cut radon levels by up to 99%, and that sealing cracks alone is not recommended as a fix ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_consumers_guide_to_radon_reduction.pdf)). DEP says installation usually takes less than a day.
4. **Budget for it.** DEP says mitigation systems typically cost $800 to $1,200 in Pennsylvania ([PA DEP, 2026](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/newsroom/2026-01-12-dep-encourages-pennsylvanians-to-test-their-homes-for-radon-during-radon-action-month)). Our [radon mitigation cost guide](https://radonroster.com/radon-mitigation-cost/) breaks down what changes the price.
5. **Retest.** DEP says to test again after the system has run at least 24 hours, and then every two years ([PA DEP](https://www.pa.gov/agencies/dep/programs-and-services/radiation-protection/radon-division/radon-in-the-home)).

Fixing radon is worth doing even if you've lived with it for years: EPA says lowering the level reduces your lung cancer risk regardless of how long the problem existed ([EPA](https://www.epa.gov/sites/default/files/2016-12/documents/2016_a_citizens_guide_to_radon.pdf)).

PA DEP Radon Hotline: 800-237-2366.

## Radon FAQ

### Is radon gas dangerous?

Yes, over long periods. Breathing radon over years can cause lung cancer. EPA estimates it causes about 21,000 U.S. lung cancer deaths a year, making it the second leading cause after smoking and the leading cause among non-smokers.

### Does radon have a smell or cause symptoms?

No. Radon has no color, smell or taste, and it causes no immediate symptoms. CDC notes most people have no symptoms until lung cancer is advanced, which is why testing matters.

### What causes radon in a house?

Mostly the soil underneath. Uranium in rock and soil breaks down into radon gas, and because indoor air pressure is usually lower than the pressure in the soil, the house draws that gas in through cracks, joints, gaps around pipes, sumps and other openings. Well water and building materials can add smaller amounts.

### Can a house without a basement have radon?

Yes. CDC and EPA say radon can build up in any home, new or old, sealed or drafty, with or without a basement. Slab-on-grade and crawlspace homes should be tested too.

### What is a safe radon level?

There is no known safe level. EPA recommends fixing a home at 4 pCi/L or higher and considering a fix between 2 and 4 pCi/L. The average U.S. indoor level is about 1.3 pCi/L.

### Does opening windows get rid of radon?

Only briefly. EPA's Consumer's Guide says that once windows and doors are closed, radon usually returns to its earlier level within about 12 hours, so ventilation is a temporary measure, not a fix.

### Can radon come from well water?

Yes. Radon can dissolve in groundwater, and most of the risk comes from radon released into the air when you shower or use water. In most homes, radon from soil is a much larger risk than radon from water, so test the air first.

### How common is radon in Pennsylvania?

Very. PA DEP estimates about 40% of homes tested are above 4 pCi/L. In DEP's public data, 37% of basement tests statewide were at or above 4 pCi/L, and in 22 of 67 counties half or more were.
