How much does radon mitigation cost in Pennsylvania?

Short answer: PA DEP says a radon mitigation system in Pennsylvania typically costs $800 to $1,200 (PA DEP, 2026). Nationally, HomeAdvisor's 2026 cost guide puts the average at $1,028, with most homeowners paying $786 to $1,274 and a full range of $395 to $2,453. After installation, expect a small rise in your electric bill, a retest every two years, and a fan replacement every several years.

How to read the numbers on this page. Each figure is labeled with where it comes from. PA figures come from Pennsylvania DEP. National figures come from HomeAdvisor's 2026 cost guide (based on its customer survey) or from EPA's January 2010 Consumer's Guide to Radon Reduction, the last EPA edition with dollar amounts, so those prices are 16 years old. None of them are quotes. Last updated October 31, 2026.

What affects the price of radon mitigation?

EPA says cost depends on the size and design of your home and on which radon reduction methods it needs (EPA Consumer's Guide, 2016). In practice, that comes down to these factors:

  • Foundation type. Basement, slab-on-grade and crawlspace homes use different systems. Many homes mix them, such as a basement under part of the house and a slab or crawlspace under the rest, and EPA notes these may need a combination of techniques.
  • What's under the slab. The number and placement of suction pipes depends on how easily air moves through the gravel or soil under the floor and on how strong the radon source is. EPA says a single suction point is often enough.
  • Diagnostic testing. If a visual inspection isn't enough, the contractor may run smoke or soil-communication tests. EPA notes that many contractors give free estimates but may charge for diagnostics.
  • Pipe route and fan location. The exhaust has to vent above the roof, and the fan can't sit in or below living space, so it usually goes in an attic, garage or on the outside wall (EPA). An interior route through closets and an exterior route up the side of the house involve different labor and materials.
  • Sealing, sump covers and crawlspace membranes. Sealing cracks makes a system work better and is part of most jobs, but EPA and DEP don't recommend sealing on its own.
  • Your starting radon level. EPA's 2010 guide lists the initial radon level alongside home size and foundation type as a factor in choosing the method.
  • Appearance and extras. EPA says options such as an extended warranty, a service plan or a less visible installation can add to the price, and its 2010 guide notes cosmetic work can push costs above the typical ranges.
  • Permits and electrical work. HomeAdvisor reports permits at $25 to $150 nationally and says the fan's electrical work is likely to need additional permits and inspections.
  • Location. HomeAdvisor says local cost of living and the number of available specialists affect price. In Pennsylvania, only DEP-certified mitigators may do the work for the public (25 Pa. Code Ch. 240), and some counties have none based locally; check your county page for who serves your area.

Ask each contractor for their DEP certification number. It's shown on every listing here.

What each system type is

  • Active subslab suction (subslab depressurization): one or more pipes through the floor slab, connected to a fan that pulls radon from under the house and vents it outdoors. EPA calls it the most common and usually most reliable method.
  • Drain-tile and sump-hole suction: variations that use an existing drain-tile loop or a capped sump pit as the suction point.
  • Block-wall suction: for basements with hollow concrete-block walls, often combined with subslab suction.
  • Crawlspace submembrane suction: a high-density plastic sheet over the dirt floor, with a pipe and fan drawing radon from beneath it. EPA says this is the most effective approach for crawlspace homes.
  • Passive systems: the same piping without a fan, usually built into new homes. EPA says they're generally less effective than active suction. WHO reports passive systems can cut indoor radon by more than 50% (WHO).
  • Pressurization and HRVs: fans that push air into the basement, or ventilators that bring in outdoor air. EPA suggests pressurization only after the common techniques haven't worked, and notes HRVs can raise heating and cooling costs.

Source: EPA Consumer's Guide to Radon Reduction, 2016.

What does a radon system cost to run and maintain?

  • Electricity and conditioned air: EPA says fan systems will slightly increase your electric bill, and most systems lose some heated or cooled air (EPA, 2016). EPA's 2010 table put the yearly total for a subslab system at $50 to $200 (national). The fan has to run all the time; EPA says never turn it off.
  • Fan replacement: EPA says fans may last five years or more and that manufacturer warranties tend not to exceed five years. Its 2010 guide put a replacement at about $200 to $350, parts and labor included (national, 2010).
  • Watching the gauge: active systems must have a warning device, usually a U-tube manometer on the pipe. PA DEP says uneven fluid levels mean the fan is running; even levels mean it may be off or not working (PA DEP).
  • Retesting: PA DEP says the installer should test after the system has run at least 24 hours, and you should retest every two years, generally in the basement and preferably in the heating season. A DIY kit costs about $20 to $30 (PA). If an active system was installed in the past year, DEP offers a free long-term test kit (PA DEP). See free and low-cost test kits.
  • HRV filters: if your system uses a heat recovery ventilator, EPA's 2010 guide says filters should be changed twice a year and cost $10 to $25 each (national, 2010).
  • After remodeling: EPA says to retest after major changes, such as finishing a basement, and to ask your contractor whether the work could void a warranty.

Who pays for radon mitigation when buying or selling a home?

No Pennsylvania rule says who pays. It's negotiated between buyer and seller, and PA DEP recommends putting it in the sales agreement.

  • Write it into the contract. PA DEP's Home Buyer's and Seller's Guide suggests the agreement spell out how the buyer and seller will share test results and test costs, and when radon mitigation will be done and who will pay for it.
  • If a test comes back high during the sale, DEP says the buyer and seller should discuss the timing and cost of the fix.
  • Sellers: fixing before listing gives you more time. DEP says it is better to correct a radon problem before the home goes on the market.
  • Disclosure. Pennsylvania law requires sellers to disclose the results of any known radon testing (Real Estate Seller Disclosure Act, per the DEP guide). See our PA real estate radon disclosure page.
  • Selling a home that already has a system: EPA says to tell buyers and give them the system's operating and maintenance information, and to check whether the warranty or guarantee transfers to the new owner (EPA).
  • Does a radon system hurt resale? EPA calls that a myth: where radon problems have been fixed, home sales have not been blocked, and the added protection is sometimes a good selling point (EPA Citizen's Guide).

For test timing and who should run the test during a sale, see radon testing when buying or selling in PA.

How do I compare radon mitigation quotes?

EPA advises weighing what you get for the money: a less expensive system may cost more to operate and maintain and may look less tidy, a more expensive system may be the best fit for your home, and the quality of materials affects how long the system lasts. Ask each contractor whether the quote includes:

  • Proof of DEP certification (shown as cert IDs on every listing here)
  • Liability insurance and any required local licenses
  • Diagnostic testing before design and installation
  • A warning device that signals if the system stops working
  • Testing after installation
  • A guarantee to reach 4 pCi/L or below, and for how long
  • The total cost including taxes and permit fees, deposit and payment schedule
  • Whether warranties or guarantees transfer if you sell the home

Source: EPA Consumer's Guide to Radon Reduction, 2016.

Frequently asked questions

How much does radon mitigation cost in Pennsylvania?

PA DEP says a typical radon system in Pennsylvania costs $800 to $1,200. Nationally, HomeAdvisor's 2026 cost guide reports an average of $1,028, with most homeowners paying $786 to $1,274 and a full range of $395 to $2,453. Get written quotes from several DEP-certified mitigators.

What makes one radon system cost more than another?

EPA says cost depends on the size and design of the home and the mitigation method needed. Foundation type, how easily air moves under the slab, the number of suction points, pipe routing, sealing work, crawlspace membranes and permits all affect the price.

How much does it cost to run a radon fan?

EPA says fan systems slightly increase your electric bill. Its 2010 guide estimated $50 to $200 a year for a typical subslab system, including fan electricity and lost heated or cooled air. That is an older national figure, so your cost may differ.

How much does radon fan replacement cost?

EPA's 2010 guide put a fan replacement at about $200 to $350 including parts and labor (national, 2010). EPA says fans may last five years or more, and manufacturer warranties tend not to exceed five years.

Do I need to retest after radon mitigation?

Yes. PA DEP says the installer should test after the system has run for at least 24 hours, and you should retest every two years, preferably during the heating season. DEP offers a free long-term test kit if an active system was installed in the past year.

Who pays for radon mitigation when selling a house in Pennsylvania?

It's negotiated. PA DEP recommends the sales agreement state when mitigation will be done and who will pay, and says buyer and seller should discuss timing and cost if a test comes back high during the sale. Pennsylvania sellers must disclose known radon test results.

Can I just seal cracks instead of installing a system?

EPA and PA DEP do not recommend sealing alone. EPA says sealing by itself has not been shown to lower radon significantly or consistently, though it is part of most mitigation systems.

How much does a radon test cost?

PA DEP says do-it-yourself kits cost about $20 to $30. HomeAdvisor reports professional tests at $150 to $800 nationally. Pennsylvania residents who have never tested may qualify for a free kit.

Is radon mitigation cheaper in a new home?

Usually. PA DEP's Home Buyer's and Seller's Guide says passive radon-resistant features cost $350 to $500 during construction, and EPA's 2010 guide said a fan can be added to a passive system later for about $300.
